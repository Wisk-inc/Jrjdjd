NatureV1

An open-source AI weather and hurricane model, made in Jamaica. Its attention works in kilometres on the real sphere, not in cells on a flat grid. It rolls the whole atmosphere forward three days and, for a hurricane, forecasts track, intensity, rapid intensification and landfall — every number with an uncertainty attached.

View on Hugging Face See the results

Packages: naturev1 0.10.0 · ihelix 0.5.1 · Python ≥ 3.10 · Developer: Nathan · Origin: Jamaica

NatureV1 is an 89.45-million-parameter AI model of the atmosphere built from scratch by CorX Labs in Jamaica, and released. It reads the last 30 hours of reanalysis — six frames, 89 channels, a 1.5° global grid — rolls the whole atmosphere forward to three days, and, for a tropical cyclone, says where it will go, how strong it will get, whether it will intensify rapidly and whether it will come ashore. Every number it produces carries an uncertainty.

On data it never trained on, its hurricane track beats a physics-only steering track and straight-line extrapolation at every lead — 163 km at 24 h and 739 km at 72 on the held-out 2017, 2019 and 2021 seasons — and on the unseen 2023 El Niño season it beat steering for every storm. It is also 2.5–4.5× less accurate than the National Hurricane Center, it underestimates strong storms, and it cannot warn of explosive intensification.

Two design decisions carry most of the weight. The engine, iHELIX, gives every attention head a physical radius in kilometres over great-circle distance, so the parameters go on the atmosphere rather than on rediscovering the planet. And because best-track labels are desperately scarce, the backbone is frozen before the hurricane heads train: about 1.2 million parameters ever see a label, on top of a backbone that learned from about 2×1011 values in reanalysis.

Trained and scored — a research model, not an operational forecast. The first full run is complete and every number on this page was measured on data the model never trained on. A red-team pass found failure modes worth knowing before you use any output; they are listed below. Do not use NatureV1 for real decisions. For a live storm, use the National Hurricane Center.

The storm’s environment is read out of the model’s own atmosphere: deep-layer steering, 200–850 hPa shear, mid-level humidity and ocean temperature — the same quantities operational statistical-dynamical intensity schemes such as SHIPS are built on. They were handed to the storm heads explicitly to help intensity. The red team shows they barely move it: the intensity forecast leans on the storm’s current wind instead.

2. The engine: attention over physical space

Most weather transformers flatten the globe into a sequence of tokens and let the model learn geography from data. iHELIX keeps the geometry instead. A domain is composed from spherical, periodic, linear and logarithmic axes: Geometry.globe() is the Earth's surface, and Geometry.atmosphere() is a spherical shell whose vertical axis is log-pressure — so 1000→500 hPa is the same distance as 500→250 hPa, which is what the atmosphere actually thinks.

The practical consequence of “FieldGrid is data, not architecture” is that the same trained model reads ERA5's regular 240 × 121 grid, a 4,096-point Fibonacci mesh, or the irregular pixel footprints of a satellite scan. The grid is an argument, not a rebuild.

3. How a storm forecast is assembled

A 1.5° grid cell is about 165 km across. It cannot see an eyewall, and it does not know how fast the storm was moving twelve hours ago. So the storm path does not ask the mesh to carry everything: it pools the mesh twice, and then hands the heads the things a coarse reanalysis cannot show.

Everything new starts at exactly zero effect. The state head opens as persistence, the ensemble noise as nothing, the environment inputs as nothing. That is not a detail of initialisation — it is what makes it safe to add a capability to a model that is already training. Adding one can never make an existing checkpoint worse, and uncertainties open at climatological spread rather than at a confident guess.

4. Training, and the argument behind it

There are on the order of 55,000 Atlantic best-track points in all of recorded history, and no engineering makes more. Several of the fields are far thinner than that: radius of maximum wind only entered HURDAT2 with the 2021 season, and quadrant wind radii only go back to 2004. Spending 89 million parameters on that many labels would be a way of memorising the Atlantic.

So the labels are spent on as few parameters as possible, and everything else is learned from reanalysis, which is effectively free.

The three training stages of the released run: what trained, how many steps, on what data
Stage What trained Steps Data and objective
1 — Pretraining Backbone and surface heads 7,275 (8 epochs) ERA5 2011–2015, every surface channel at every lead; area-weighted Gaussian NLL over roughly 2×1011 values
1b — Rollout Backbone and state head 4,000 Curriculum to 12 steps (72 h), 2-member CRPS ensemble, mass conserved — 74.3 GPU-hours
2 — Storm heads ~1.2M parameters only — backbone frozen Best epoch 3 of 28 HURDAT2, 1979 onward: 584 storms, 16,219 samples; early-stopped on held-out seasons, then consensus weights fitted on them

About one week end to end, on one GPU. The run used a single RTX PRO 6000 (96 GB) in a hosted notebook. GPU time was roughly three to four days; the rest was session overhead — sessions end after 12 hours or 90 idle minutes and the disk is wiped, so every restart re-pulled checkpoints and staged data from the Hugging Face repository before training resumed. Every stage resumes from a one-minute checkpoint, so no restart lost more than a minute of training. A machine that keeps its disk would cut the whole run to three or four days.

Freezing the backbone has a practical payoff too: it runs once per storm into a cached feature bank (about two hours), after which the heads train in minutes.

Splitting data so it cannot cheat

  • ERA5 is split by time over 1959–2021: train to 2015, validate 2016–2019, test 2020–2021. This run staged the last five training years, 2011–2015 (7,300 global states, about 38 GB), and validated on 2016. A random split would put tomorrow’s answer in the training set.
  • HURDAT2 is split by season. Storm heads trained on 1979–2016; 2017, 2019 and 2021 are held out for validation and scoring, and 2018, 2020 and 2022 for testing. Splitting by storm would leak, because storms in a season share an environment.
  • Labels are paired by timestamp, not row index. HURDAT2 carries off-synoptic entries for landfalls and intensity maxima; those are dropped rather than snapped to the nearest analysis hour.
  • Scarce fields arrive as NaN and are masked, never imputed. Radius of maximum wind exists on under 5% of points.
  • A data-leak audit found none. The held-out and test seasons never appear in training, the weather backbone saw only 2011–2015, and 2023 is outside everything.

5. What it refuses to do

This is as much a part of the model as anything above it. A forecast that cannot say I don't know is not a cautious forecast — it is a dangerous one.

  • An untrained head is never shown. An output whose head received zero training steps is replaced by {"available": false, "reason": …}. In this release that covers weather type, landfall timing, rapid-intensification onset, ENSO, and the satellite and environment encoders. The training record travels inside the checkpoint.
  • An untrained input path is marked untrustworthy. A forecast run from the satellite path comes back with trustworthy: false, and the live satellite forecast is refused outright.
  • Every forecast is checked against physical bounds — and the check reports, it does not block. check_forecast tests every scenario track, intensity, eyewall and rapid-intensification value: no 400 kt hurricane, no pressure outside 850–1100 hPa, no cone wider than an ocean, no probabilities that fail to sum to one. A caller must act on what it returns. The red team lists what it does not catch.
  • The publishing gate is weak, so this release was reviewed by hand. The pipeline publishes only if the model beats persistence somewhere on held-out data and changes its answer when the weather changes — a bar precipitation alone can clear. This release went out after the full scorecards and the red team below were reviewed.

The scoring follows what operational centres do: area-weighted RMSE and anomaly correlation against persistence and climatology on identical batches, in physical units, with a 1% margin before anything is called skill. The storm scorecard adds the baselines a forecaster would really reach for — a storm that stays put, straight extrapolation, and the physics-only steering track — and scores the consensus leave-one-season-out. Beating extrapolation and persistence is the bar. Beating steering is what the learned part is for.

6. Results

Every number here comes from the published weights, on data the model never trained on. Track errors are great-circle kilometres. “Steering” is the physics-only steering-flow track (no training), “straight” is extrapolation of the last 12 hours, “stay-put” is a storm that does not move, and wind “persistence” is “it stays as strong as it is now”. The raw outputs and the scripts that produced them are in the Hugging Face repository.

Global weather

  • The full-state rollout beats climatology by more than 1% on 84 of 84 field–lead pairs, and has skill against both persistence and climatology out to +72 h on sea-level pressure, 700 hPa humidity, 2 m temperature, 850 hPa temperature, 10 m wind and 500 hPa geopotential. Its ensemble spread/skill ratio at +24 h is 1.08, where 1.0 is perfectly calibrated.
  • The direct multi-lead surface heads have no skill — they beat persistence on 6 of 45 field–lead pairs, all precipitation. They were trained in stage one and not updated when the rollout retrained the backbone underneath them. Use the rollout for weather.

In context

For scale only: the National Hurricane Center’s official Atlantic track error averaged about 66 km at 24 h and 168 km at 72 h over 2018–2022, and the leading global AI models — GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, FuXi — and ECMWF are in the same league. NatureV1’s 163 km and 739 km are 2.5–4.5× larger.

That gap has a plain cause. NatureV1 is a 1.5° model trained on five years of data with a few GPU-days of compute; those systems use 0.25°, decades of data and orders of magnitude more compute. Intensity is the weakest output: like every 1.5° model it cannot see an eyewall, and it forecasts about 70 kt for storms that reach 145–160 kt.

Red team: where it breaks

A deliberate attempt to break the published model: physically impossible inputs, calibration against what actually happened, removing one input at a time, logical consistency, a data-leak audit, and the hardest storm that could be found. Everything is on held-out data unless noted. The data-leak audit found nothing.

Inputs the safety checks do not catch

  • Garbage in, plausible out. Random noise in place of the backbone features produced a normal-looking forecast — 22–45 kt and a sensible track — with zero check failures. There is no out-of-distribution detector.
  • A corrupted steering prior passes straight through. Shifting it by 30° moved the forecast 30°, with zero check failures.
  • The consensus track is not bounds-checked — a centre near the pole gave consensus latitudes above 90°; only the scenario tracks were flagged.
  • A missing (NaN) storm centre is flagged on the track, but intensity and rapid intensification are still produced as if valid.
  • Caught correctly: a 400 kt current wind (forecast 370 kt, flagged), positions off the planet, cones wider than an ocean, and the untrained satellite path. The dateline and Southern Hemisphere beta drift are handled correctly.
  • A bug: build_forecast without a training record crashes on outputs that carry no ENSO field.

Logical consistency

  • Landfall is the probability of landfall by a lead, which can never fall as the lead grows. It falls in 20% of forecasts, by up to 12 points.
  • A 50 or 64 kt wind radius exceeds the 34/50 kt radius in 21% of forecasts, and the radius of maximum wind exceeds the 34 kt radius in 17% — both physically impossible.
  • Consistent: the three rapid-intensification thresholds are always ordered, and the two intensity heads agree to 1.5 kt on average.

The hardest case: Hurricane Otis, 2023

East Pacific — outside the Atlantic the storm heads trained on — run through the full model from raw ERA5, five forecasts. Otis went from 50 kt to a 130 kt Category 5 in 24 hours and struck Acapulco. From 24 October 00Z, NatureV1 forecast 45 kt — a slight weakening — and gave rapid intensification 44%, about what it said on the quiet days before (34–38%). Track was 104 km at 24 h against steering’s 156, but at 72 h it kept Otis at the same latitude while the storm moved 5° north. It cannot warn of explosive intensification.

Reproducibility gap. The full-state rollout scales its residuals by per-channel tendency statistics measured from the training data, and those are not shipped in this release. Reproducing the weather rollout exactly means re-measuring them from ERA5 2011–2015 (64 evenly spaced training windows).

Still running when the card was written: six more storms outside the Atlantic (West Pacific, Southern Hemisphere, East Pacific) and a 10-day rollout to test stability beyond the 72 h trained horizon. They will be added here.

What else the pipeline checks

  • A training watchdog raises on a non-finite loss and warns on divergence and collapse; held-out validation runs every 500 steps with an explicit over-fitting flag.
  • Preflight checks grid alignment, normalisation spikes, grid weights and device agreement before step one, and halts the run if anything fails.
  • A response probe confirms that different inputs give different outputs — the test that catches a model which has quietly collapsed to climatology.
  • Resilience is treated as a safety property for a multi-day run: atomic checkpoint writes every minute, optimizer and schedule state travelling with the weights, damaged checkpoints falling back to older ones, incompatible ones quarantined rather than force-loaded, and a supervised process that restarts on crash, out-of-memory or stall.

7. Limits, stated plainly

Do not use NatureV1 for real decisions. It is a research model, not an operational forecast, and no independent party has verified it. For a live storm, the National Hurricane Center is the authority.

  • Resolution. 1.5° is about 165 km, which cannot resolve an eyewall, so intensity is the weakest output. The environment inputs added to compensate barely move it.
  • Not operational. It runs on reanalysis, which is available days after the fact. There is no real-time analysis feed, and the satellite input path exists but is untrained.
  • Verification is internal. Three held-out seasons plus 2023, scored by the project’s own scripts.
  • Intensity. Biased low, by 10–18 kt for major hurricanes; misses explosive intensification entirely (Otis). Rapid-intensification probabilities are far too high, and radius of maximum wind has no skill.
  • Uncertainty display. The full scenario mixture is well calibrated, but a single scenario’s cone is over-confident and the cone_radius_km_68 field is a 39% ellipse. Draw cones from the mixture.
  • No input sanity check. Corrupted inputs can produce plausible forecasts that pass every check. Physically impossible structure — wind radii out of order, landfall probability falling with lead — is not yet caught.
  • Surface heads are stale. The direct multi-lead surface fields were not retrained after the rollout stage; use the full-state rollout for weather.
  • Small data. Five years of reanalysis at 1.5°, and the storm heads start memorising after about three epochs.
  • Atlantic only for the storm heads, 1979 onward; other basins are out of distribution.
  • Tornadoes, hail and flash floods are far below this resolution and out of scope.

8. Using it

The whole pipeline — install, staging, three training stages, scorecards, and publishing only if it earned it — is one cell, colab_naturev1.py, shipped with the package. Every setting is at the top. It runs in marimo, Colab, Jupyter or a plain script.

pip install "naturev1[all]"

Load a checkpoint and ask it what it has actually been trained to do:

import torch
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from ihelix import fibonacci_sphere
from naturev1 import NatureV1, NatureConfig, build_forecast

path = hf_hub_download("Sigmandndnns/NatureV1-500", "checkpoints/stage1/latest.pt")
payload = torch.load(path, map_location="cpu", weights_only=False)
config = NatureConfig.from_dict(payload["config"])

model = NatureV1(config, fibonacci_sphere(config.latent_points,
                                          num_neighbours=config.latent_neighbours,
                                          cluster_size=config.latent_cluster))
model.load_state_dict(payload["model"])

print(model.trained_heads())   # steps each output has received; 0 = never trained, never shown

build_forecast turns a forward pass into the forecast bundle — track scenarios with cones, the consensus and its members, landfall, intensity, rapid intensification, eyewall structure and a point forecast — in physical units with 90% intervals, with every untrained section marked unavailable:

bundle = build_forecast(
    outputs,
    config.lead_times_hours,
    issued,
    storm_center=(lat, lon),
    trained=model.trained_heads(),
)

9. Why it is built this way

Three convictions run through the whole stack, and each one is a bet about where the parameters should go.

Geometry is not something to learn

Distances, areas, the sphere, the vertical: these are known. Giving attention physical radii and true area weights spends the parameters on the atmosphere instead of on rediscovering the globe.

Scarce labels buy few parameters

About 55,000 Atlantic best-track points exist and no engineering makes more. The backbone learns from free reanalysis; about 1.2 million parameters learn from the labels, with the physics handed to them explicitly.

A forecast must be able to say “I don't know”

Every output carries an uncertainty, every untrained head is hidden, and the release was red-teamed — with what broke published alongside what worked.

The engine is not a one-off either. iHELIX grew out of HELIX, the linear-context language architecture published as helix-lm — the temporal strand that carries state across the six input frames is the same gated delta-rule recurrence, applied to time instead of to text.

10. Made in Jamaica, for the hurricane belt

NatureV1 is an open-source AI weather and hurricane model built in Jamaica, an island that sits squarely in the Atlantic hurricane belt. Its storm heads were trained on HURDAT2, the Atlantic best-track record, which covers the Caribbean Sea and the Gulf of Mexico as well as the open ocean — the same storms that reach the Caribbean. Its held-out tests include storms that crossed the islands, among them Irma and Maria in 2017 and Franklin and Tammy in 2023.

AI forecasting already matters here. When Hurricane Melissa made landfall in Jamaica in October 2025, the National Hurricane Center drew on Google DeepMind’s WeatherNext for its forecast of the storm’s rapid intensification. NatureV1 is not that kind of system. It is a 1.5° research model built by one person, and its own red team shows it cannot warn of explosive intensification — the kind of rapid strengthening that makes storms like Melissa so dangerous.

What it offers instead is openness: a Jamaican-built weather model whose weights, code, results and failure modes are all public, that anyone can download, re-run, retrain or try to break. How it was built is written up in Building a hurricane AI in Jamaica, on one GPU.

For warnings in Jamaica, follow the Meteorological Service of Jamaica and the Office of Disaster Preparedness and Emergency Management (ODPEM). For the wider Atlantic, the National Hurricane Center. NatureV1 is not a substitute for any of them.

11. Questions

What is NatureV1?

An 89.45M-parameter probabilistic model of the atmosphere, released under Apache-2.0. Given the last 30 hours of ERA5 reanalysis it rolls the whole atmosphere forward to 72 hours and, for a tropical cyclone, forecasts its track as weighted scenarios, its intensity, the chance of rapid intensification and the chance of landfall — every number with an uncertainty. The first full run is complete and it has been scored on data it never trained on. It is a research model, not an operational forecast.

Is there a Jamaican AI weather model?

Yes. NatureV1 is an open-source AI weather and hurricane model built in Jamaica by CorX Labs, an independent AI lab founded by Nathan. It rolls the global atmosphere forward three days and, for hurricanes, forecasts their track, intensity, rapid intensification and landfall, each with an uncertainty. It is free under Apache-2.0 on Hugging Face and PyPI. It is a research model rather than an operational forecast, so for official forecasts and warnings in Jamaica, use the Meteorological Service of Jamaica.

Can NatureV1 forecast hurricanes that affect Jamaica and the Caribbean?

It covers the whole Atlantic basin, including the Caribbean Sea, because its storm heads were trained on the Atlantic best-track record from 1979 onward, and it was tested on storms that crossed the Caribbean islands, such as Maria in 2017 and Franklin and Tammy in 2023. But it cannot be used for a live storm: it runs on reanalysis published days after the fact, its track errors are 2.5–4.5× the National Hurricane Center’s, it underestimates strong storms, and it cannot warn of explosive intensification. In Jamaica, follow the Meteorological Service of Jamaica and ODPEM.

Can I use NatureV1 to forecast a real storm?

No. It is a research model, not an operational forecast. It runs on reanalysis, which is published days after the fact, so there is no real-time path. Its track errors are 2.5–4.5× the National Hurricane Center’s, it underestimates strong storms by 10–18 kt, its rapid-intensification probabilities are far too high, and it missed Hurricane Otis’s explosive intensification entirely. Its checks report problems rather than block them, and corrupted inputs can produce plausible-looking forecasts. For a live storm, use the National Hurricane Center.

How accurate is NatureV1 on hurricanes?

On 59 Atlantic storms from the held-out 2017, 2019 and 2021 seasons, its consensus track error was 163 km at 24 h, 421 at 48, 739 at 72 and 1,373 at 120 — better than a physics-only steering track and straight-line extrapolation at every lead. Its wind forecast beat persistence from 24 h on. On the unseen 2023 El Niño season it scored 167 km at 24 h and 810 at 72, and beat steering for all seven hurricanes at 24, 48 and 72 h. For scale, the NHC’s official error averaged about 66 km at 24 h and 168 at 72 over 2018–2022, so NatureV1’s errors are 2.5–4.5× larger. The full tables are above.

How does NatureV1 compare with GraphCast and other AI weather models?

It is far smaller and less accurate. The leading AI weather models — GraphCast, Pangu-Weather and FuXi — and the ECMWF forecast are in the same league as the National Hurricane Center’s official track forecasts, which averaged about 66 km of error at 24 h and 168 km at 72 over 2018–2022. NatureV1’s 163 km and 739 km are 2.5–4.5× larger. Those systems run at 0.25° with decades of data and orders of magnitude more compute; NatureV1 is a 1.5° model trained on five years of data with a few GPU-days.

How do AI weather models work?

An AI weather model learns how the atmosphere changes from decades of past weather — usually the ERA5 reanalysis — instead of solving the physics equations step by step. Given the current state of the atmosphere it predicts the state a few hours later, then feeds that prediction back in to step further ahead. NatureV1 works this way, six hours at a time out to 72 hours. What is different is the engine: its attention heads work in physical kilometres over the curved Earth rather than on a flat grid, and its hurricane forecasts start from a physics-only steering track and learn a correction on top.

What is geodesic attention, and why does it matter for weather?

Most weather transformers flatten the globe into tokens and leave the model to work out geography from data. Here every head is given a physical radius in kilometres, with compact support, and attends over great-circle distance — 120 km for fronts and convection, up to 1,600 km for synoptic scale. Points carry true area weights and a local east/north/up frame, so “20 km north” means the same thing at the equator and at 80° latitude.

Why is the backbone frozen when the hurricane heads train?

Because the labels are scarce in a way no engineering fixes. The whole Atlantic best-track record is on the order of 55,000 points, and the rarest fields are far thinner — radius of maximum wind only entered HURDAT2 with the 2021 season, quadrant wind radii only go back to 2004. So the backbone learns from roughly 2×1011 values in reanalysis, then freezes, and only about 1.2 million parameters ever see a label: 16,219 samples from 584 storms. Even then the heads start memorising quickly — the best checkpoint was epoch 3 of 28.

What does it mean that the track head predicts a residual?

Because the difference from a steering track is a much easier target than the track itself. A physics-only steering track — the path a storm takes if it is simply carried by the thickness-weighted 850–200 hPa flow around it, plus beta drift — needs no training at all, so the learned part only fixes what steering gets wrong. The red team measured the split: removing the steering prior raises the 24 h track error by 240%, and the learned correction on top of it is worth about 30% at 24 h.

What is the consensus track?

The learned track, a physics-only steering track and straight extrapolation, blended with per-lead weights fitted on held-out seasons. It copies something operational forecasting established long ago — consensus aids routinely beat every individual model that goes into them — and it holds here: scored leave-one-season-out on 2017, 2019 and 2021, the consensus had the lowest track error at every lead, ahead of the learned track alone. Every member is reported alongside the blend.

What does NatureV1 refuse to do, and what still gets through?

An output whose head never received a training step is not shown at all — weather type and landfall timing, among others, come back as unavailable. A forecast from the untrained satellite path is flagged untrustworthy, and the live satellite forecast is refused. Every scenario track, intensity, eyewall and rapid-intensification value is checked against physical bounds — but the check reports problems rather than blocking them. The red team found what gets through: random noise in place of the backbone features produced a normal-looking forecast with zero check failures, a corrupted steering prior passed straight through, and the consensus track is not bounds-checked. There is no out-of-distribution detector.

Is NatureV1 free and open source?

Yes. NatureV1 and its engine, iHELIX, are released under Apache-2.0, which allows commercial use. The weights, raw results and evaluation scripts are on Hugging Face, and the code installs with pip install "naturev1[all]". One reproducibility gap is stated openly: the per-channel tendency statistics the weather rollout needs are not shipped and have to be re-measured from ERA5.

Who built NatureV1, and how long did training take?

Nathan, the founder of CorX Labs and a solo developer from Jamaica, built the whole stack: the iHELIX engine, the model, the training pipeline and the evaluation. Training took about one week of wall-clock time on a single RTX PRO 6000 GPU in a hosted notebook — three to four days of GPU time, the rest lost to session restarts. The full-atmosphere rollout stage alone took 74.3 GPU-hours. The full story is on the blog.
