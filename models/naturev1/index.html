NatureV1

A probabilistic model of the atmosphere whose attention works in kilometres on the real sphere, not in cells on a flat grid. Five-day global weather, a full-atmosphere rollout, and — for a tropical cyclone — track, intensity, rapid intensification and landfall, every number with an uncertainty attached.

View on Hugging Face How the engine works

Packages: naturev1 0.10.0 · ihelix 0.5.1 · Python ≥ 3.10 · Developer: Nathan · Origin: Jamaica

NatureV1 is an 89.18-million-parameter model of the atmosphere built from scratch by CorX Labs in Jamaica. It reads the last 30 hours of reanalysis — six frames, 89 channels, a 1.5° global grid — and forecasts global surface weather out to five days, steps the whole atmosphere forward to three, and, for a tropical cyclone, says where it will go, how strong it will get, whether it will intensify rapidly and whether it will come ashore. Every number it produces carries an uncertainty.

Two design decisions carry most of the weight. The engine underneath, iHELIX, gives every attention head a physical radius in kilometres over great-circle distance, so the parameters are spent on the atmosphere rather than on rediscovering the shape of the planet. And because best-track labels are desperately scarce, the backbone is frozen before the hurricane heads train: roughly a million parameters ever see a label, on top of a backbone that learned from about 2×1011 free values in reanalysis.

Training is in progress, and no forecast skill is claimed. The Hugging Face repository holds the live checkpoints of the first full run, mirrored while it trains. The scorecards that decide whether this model is any good are produced by the training pipeline itself and do not exist yet. Do not use NatureV1 for real decisions. For a live storm, use the National Hurricane Center.

1. What it forecasts

Three kinds of output come out of one forward pass. Leads are 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96 and 120 hours.

NatureV1 outputs, grouped by what they describe
GroupOutputForm
Global weather 2 m temperature, mean sea-level pressure, 10 m wind, precipitation Mean and 90% interval at every grid point
Weather type — clear through thunderstorm to snow A category at every grid point
Full-state rollout — all 86 prognostic channels, surface to 50 hPa Stepped 6 h at a time on its own forecast to 72 h, as an ensemble, with mass conservation
Tropical cyclone Track Six weighted scenarios, each with a tilting uncertainty ellipse per lead — the cone is drawn by the model, not looked up in a table
Consensus track Learned track, physics-only steering track and extrapolation, blended with per-lead weights; every member reported alongside the blend
Intensity Maximum wind and minimum central pressure per lead, with intervals and the Saffir–Simpson category
Rapid intensification Probability of a 25, 30 and 35 kt gain in 24 h, plus the expected change
Landfall Probability by each lead, and the most likely window
Core structure Peak eyewall wind, radius of maximum wind, and 34/50/64 kt wind radii by quadrant
Anywhere A local forecast for any point on Earth Physical units, read off the gridded fields

The storm also reports its own environment, read out of the model's atmosphere rather than supplied from outside: deep-layer steering flow, 200–850 hPa shear, mid-level humidity and ocean temperature. Those are, deliberately, the same quantities that operational statistical-dynamical intensity schemes such as SHIPS are built on — climatology, persistence and a handful of environmental predictors. Handing them to the heads explicitly is cheaper than hoping a million parameters rediscover them from 55,000 labels.

2. The engine: attention over physical space

Most weather transformers flatten the globe into a sequence of tokens and let the model learn geography from data. iHELIX keeps the geometry instead. A domain is composed from spherical, periodic, linear and logarithmic axes: Geometry.globe() is the Earth's surface, and Geometry.atmosphere() is a spherical shell whose vertical axis is log-pressure — so 1000→500 hPa is the same distance as 500→250 hPa, which is what the atmosphere actually thinks.

The practical consequence of “FieldGrid is data, not architecture” is that the same trained model reads ERA5's regular 240 × 121 grid, a 4,096-point Fibonacci mesh, or the irregular pixel footprints of a satellite scan. The grid is an argument, not a rebuild.

3. How a storm forecast is assembled

A 1.5° grid cell is about 165 km across. It cannot see an eyewall, and it does not know how fast the storm was moving twelve hours ago. So the storm path does not ask the mesh to carry everything: it pools the mesh twice, and then hands the heads the things a coarse reanalysis cannot show.

Everything new starts at exactly zero effect. The state head opens as persistence, the ensemble noise as nothing, the environment inputs as nothing. That is not a detail of initialisation — it is what makes it safe to add a capability to a model that is already training. Adding one can never make an existing checkpoint worse, and uncertainties open at climatological spread rather than at a confident guess.

4. Training, and the argument behind it

There are on the order of 55,000 Atlantic best-track points in all of recorded history, and no engineering makes more. Several of the fields are far thinner than that: radius of maximum wind only entered HURDAT2 with the 2021 season, and quadrant wind radii only go back to 2004. Spending 89 million parameters on that many labels would be a way of memorising the Atlantic.

So the labels are spent on as few parameters as possible, and everything else is learned from reanalysis, which is effectively free.

The three training stages: what trains, what is frozen, and on what data
Stage What trains Data Objective
1 — Pretraining The whole backbone Years of ERA5, every surface channel at every lead Area-weighted Gaussian negative log-likelihood — roughly 2×1011 supervised values
1b — Rollout The residual state head ERA5, stepped on its own forecast Curriculum to 12 steps (72 h), 2-member CRPS ensemble, mass conserved
2 — Storm heads ~1M parameters only — the backbone is frozen HURDAT2 Atlantic best tracks, 1979 onward Early stopping on seasons never trained on; consensus weights fitted afterwards on those held-out seasons

Freezing the backbone has a second payoff that is purely practical: it can be run once per storm into a cached feature bank, after which the heads train in seconds per epoch. A design that would otherwise be slow to iterate on becomes fast to iterate on, which is how a solo project gets anywhere at all.

Splitting data so it cannot cheat

  • ERA5 is split by time — train to 2015, validate 2016–2019, test 2020–2021. A random split would put tomorrow's answer in the training set, because consecutive atmospheric states are nearly identical.
  • HURDAT2 is split by season — validate on 2017, 2019 and 2021, test on 2018 and 2020. Splitting by storm would leak, because storms in a season share an environment.
  • Labels are paired by timestamp, not row index. HURDAT2 is not strictly six-hourly: it carries off-synoptic entries for landfalls and intensity maxima. Those are dropped rather than snapped to the nearest analysis hour.
  • Scarce fields arrive as NaN and are masked, never imputed. Radius of maximum wind exists on under 5% of points; filling it in would teach the model a number nobody measured.

5. What it refuses to do

This is as much a part of the model as anything above it. A forecast that cannot say I don't know is not a cautious forecast — it is a dangerous one.

  • An untrained head is never shown. An output whose head has received zero training steps is replaced by {"available": false, "reason": …}. The training record travels inside the checkpoint, so this survives being loaded somewhere else.
  • An untrained input path is marked untrustworthy. A forecast run from a path the model was never trained on — today, the satellite path — comes back with trustworthy: false, and the live satellite forecast is refused outright.
  • Every number is bounds-checked before it is shown or saved. No 400 kt hurricane, no pressure outside 850–1100 hPa, no cone wider than an ocean, no probabilities that do not sum to one.
  • It is published only if it earned it. The pipeline publishes a checkpoint only if it beats persistence on held-out data by a margin, and only if it demonstrably changes its answer when the weather changes.

The scoring follows what operational centres actually do: area-weighted RMSE and anomaly correlation against persistence and climatology on identical batches, in physical units, with a 1% margin before anything is called skill. The storm scorecard adds the baselines a forecaster would really reach for — a storm that stays put, straight extrapolation, and the physics-only steering track — and scores the consensus leave-one-season-out.

Beating extrapolation and persistence is the bar. Beating steering is what the learned part is for.

6. What has been measured

Nothing, for skill. The first full training run is in progress. What has been verified end to end is the mechanism: staging, all three training stages, the scorecards and the hindcast run on real ERA5 and HURDAT2 data at reduced scale, with 236 tests covering the physics, the label pairing, the resilience paths and the honesty rules. When the run finishes, the scorecards go here verbatim.

The one number that does not depend on training

The steering member uses no trained parameters at all, so it can be scored today. On Hurricane Dorian, issued 2019‑08‑30 18Z from the 1.5° analysis:

Great-circle track error on Hurricane Dorian for the physics-only steering track against straight extrapolation
Lead Physics-only steering Straight extrapolation NHC official, recent seasons
24 h177 kmNot reported~60 km
48 h382 km397 km~100 km
72 h546 km651 km~145 km
120 h531 km1,012 km~230 km

Read it the right way round. Steering beats extrapolation from 48 hours onward, and the gap widens with lead — by five days it is roughly half the error. That is the expected shape: extrapolation is fine for a few hours and hopeless once a storm turns.

The NHC column is there for scale, not for comparison. Those are averages over many storms from an operational centre using every tool it has; this is a single storm, from a single 1.5° analysis, with no training. The right conclusion is that the steering member is the floor the learned model starts from, not the ceiling it is aiming at.

What else the pipeline checks

  • A training watchdog raises on a non-finite loss and warns on divergence and collapse; held-out validation runs every 500 steps with an explicit over-fitting flag.
  • Preflight checks grid alignment, normalisation spikes, grid weights and device agreement before step one, and halts the run if anything fails.
  • A response probe confirms that different inputs actually give different outputs — the test that catches a model which has quietly collapsed to climatology.
  • Resilience is treated as a safety property for a multi-day run: atomic checkpoint writes every minute, optimizer and schedule state travelling with the weights, damaged checkpoints falling back to older ones, incompatible ones quarantined rather than force-loaded, and a supervised process that restarts on crash, out-of-memory or stall.

7. Limits, stated plainly

Do not use NatureV1 for real decisions. Training is unfinished, no scorecard exists, and no independent party has verified anything. For a live storm, the National Hurricane Center is the authority.

  • Resolution. 1.5° is about 165 km, which cannot resolve an eyewall. Intensity is expected to be the weakest output; the explicit environment inputs exist to compensate, and the scorecard will say how far they get.
  • Not operational. It runs on reanalysis, which is published days after the fact. There is no real-time analysis feed, and the satellite input path exists but is untrained.
  • Verification is internal. Three validation seasons, scored by the pipeline's own scorecards. Calibration of the landfall and rapid-intensification probabilities has not been measured at all yet.
  • Atlantic only for the storm heads, 1979 onward.
  • Tornadoes, hail and flash floods are far below this resolution and out of scope.

8. Using it

The whole pipeline — install, staging, three training stages, scorecards, and publishing only if it earned it — is one cell, colab_naturev1.py, shipped with the package. Every setting is at the top. It runs in marimo, Colab, Jupyter or a plain script.

pip install "naturev1[all]"

Load a checkpoint and ask it what it has actually been trained to do:

import torch
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from ihelix import fibonacci_sphere
from naturev1 import NatureV1, NatureConfig, build_forecast

path = hf_hub_download("Sigmandndnns/NatureV1-500", "checkpoints/stage1/latest.pt")
payload = torch.load(path, map_location="cpu", weights_only=False)
config = NatureConfig.from_dict(payload["config"])

model = NatureV1(config, fibonacci_sphere(config.latent_points,
                                          num_neighbours=config.latent_neighbours,
                                          cluster_size=config.latent_cluster))
model.load_state_dict(payload["model"])

print(model.trained_heads())   # steps each output has received; 0 = never trained, never shown

build_forecast turns a forward pass into the forecast bundle — track scenarios with cones, the consensus and its members, landfall, intensity, rapid intensification, eyewall structure and a point forecast — in physical units with 90% intervals, with every untrained section marked unavailable:

bundle = build_forecast(
    outputs,
    config.lead_times_hours,
    issued,
    storm_center=(lat, lon),
    trained=model.trained_heads(),
)

9. Why it is built this way

Three convictions run through the whole stack, and each one is a bet about where the parameters should go.

Geometry is not something to learn

Distances, areas, the sphere, the vertical: these are known. Giving attention physical radii and true area weights spends the parameters on the atmosphere instead of on rediscovering the globe.

Scarce labels buy few parameters

About 55,000 Atlantic best-track points exist and no engineering makes more. The backbone learns from free reanalysis; a million parameters learn from the labels, with the physics handed to them explicitly.

A forecast must be able to say “I don't know”

Every output carries an uncertainty, every untrained head is hidden, every number is bounds-checked, and nothing is believed until it beats persistence.

The engine is not a one-off either. iHELIX grew out of HELIX, the linear-context language architecture published as helix-lm — the temporal strand that carries state across the six input frames is the same gated delta-rule recurrence, applied to time instead of to text.

10. Questions

What is NatureV1, in one paragraph?

An 89.18M-parameter probabilistic model of the atmosphere. Given the last 30 hours of ERA5 reanalysis it forecasts global surface weather to five days, rolls the whole atmosphere forward to 72 hours, and — for a tropical cyclone — predicts where it goes, how strong it gets, whether it intensifies rapidly and whether it makes landfall, every number with an uncertainty. The first full training run is in progress and no forecast skill is claimed yet.

Can I use it to forecast a real storm?

No. Training has not finished, no scorecard exists, and nothing about its skill has been established. It also runs on reanalysis, which is published days after the fact, so there is no real-time path even once it is trained. For a live storm, use the National Hurricane Center.

What is geodesic attention, and why does it matter here?

Most weather transformers flatten the globe into tokens and leave the model to work out geography from data. Here every head is given a physical radius in kilometres, with compact support, and attends over great-circle distance — 120 km for fronts and convection, up to 1,600 km for synoptic scale. Points carry true area weights and a local east/north/up frame, so “20 km north” means the same thing at the equator and at 80° latitude.

Why freeze the backbone before training the hurricane heads?

Because the labels are scarce in a way no engineering fixes. The whole Atlantic best-track record is on the order of 55,000 points, and the rarest fields are far thinner — radius of maximum wind only entered HURDAT2 with the 2021 season, quadrant wind radii only go back to 2004. So the backbone learns from roughly 2×1011 free values in reanalysis, then freezes, and only about a million parameters ever see a label. It also makes iteration fast: the frozen backbone runs once per storm into a cached feature bank, after which the heads train in seconds per epoch.

Why does the track head predict a residual?

Because the difference from a steering track is a much easier target than the track itself. A physics-only steering track — the path a storm takes if it is simply carried by the thickness-weighted 850–200 hPa flow around it, plus climatological beta drift — needs no training at all. The learned part is then only responsible for what steering gets wrong: beta drift anomalies, trough interactions, recurvature timing. It also means the model has a sensible answer before it has learned much.

What is the consensus track, and why three members?

The learned track, the steering track and straight extrapolation are blended with per-lead weights fitted on held-out seasons. This copies something operational forecasting established long ago: consensus aids routinely beat every individual model that goes into them. Every member is reported alongside the blend, so you can see what the consensus was made of rather than taking one number on trust.

What does the model refuse to do?

An output whose head has never received a training step is not shown at all — it comes back as unavailable, and the training record travels inside the checkpoint. A forecast from an input path that was never trained on, such as the satellite path today, is flagged untrustworthy, and the live satellite forecast is refused outright. Every number is bounds-checked first: no 400 kt hurricane, no pressure outside 850–1100 hPa, no probabilities that fail to sum to one. And a checkpoint is published only if it beats persistence on held-out data by a margin.

Has anything been measured yet?

One thing, and only because it uses no trained parameters. On Hurricane Dorian, issued 2019‑08‑30 18Z, the physics-only steering track had errors of 177 km at 24 h, 382 at 48, 546 at 72 and 531 at 120 — beating straight extrapolation from 48 h onward (397, 651, 1012 km). That is a result about the floor, not about the learned model. Everything else verified so far is mechanism: the whole pipeline runs end to end on real data at reduced scale, with 236 tests over the physics, the label pairing, the resilience paths and the honesty rules.
